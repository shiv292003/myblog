A blank notebook makes a surprisingly large promise. It suggests that the next thing you write will be worth keeping.

That can make it difficult to begin.

This sample essay proposes a smaller promise: the page only needs to hold what you're thinking about today. It doesn't need to prove that you understand it.

## The page before the answer

There is a kind of writing that happens before an idea is ready for an audience. Half a sentence. A diagram with an arrow pointing nowhere. A question you keep returning to because none of the answers quite fit.

It is easy to treat those pages as waste. But they are often where the useful work happens.

> A notebook isn't a museum of things you already know. It is somewhere to leave the door open.

## Three small experiments

1. **Keep the question.** Write it down before trying to sound clever about the answer.
2. **Draw the confusing part.** A box and an arrow sometimes say more than a paragraph.
3. **Return a week later.** Notice what changed, including what no longer seems important.

None of this requires a special notebook. A text file works. So does the back of an envelope.

### A small distinction worth making

Being unfinished is not the same as being careless. You can be precise about what you don't know. You can name an assumption, mark a guess, or leave yourself a note that says, quite simply, *check this*.

That kind of honesty makes a page useful to your future self.

## A place to stop

The end of a note doesn't need to be the end of a thought. Sometimes the best last sentence is a question to pick up another day.
